## Byline

There's a framing I've been sitting with lately that I think cuts closer to the truth about where AI engineering is heading than most takes out there: **the real frontier isn't writing better LLM prompts — it's designing the harness around the model.**

I published a piece on this recently, and the core idea is that Agentic Harnessing is genuinely hard in a way that software engineering is quickly ceasing to be. LLMs are already close to "solved" for verifiable tasks — give them a spec and a test suite and they'll iterate toward it. But building the environment in which an agent reliably operates? That's still a deeply human problem, and it's one that AI can't yet bootstrap itself out of.

Here's what Harness Engineering actually involves:

- **Context Engineering** — deciding what information the agent sees and when, so it stays sharp and doesn't get polluted by noise
- **Feedback Loops** — feeding activity back to the agent to compound knowledge and prevent the same mistakes repeating
- **Advanced Tooling** — targeted scripts and MCPs that introduce determinism into an inherently non-deterministic model
- **Structured Paths** — defining explicit state transitions so an agent moves with intent instead of wandering

The line that stuck with me most while writing this: *"The harness is what bridges the gap between a raw model and a sophisticated, reliable digital employee."*

Software engineers are starting to feel like the hard part of their job is disappearing. What's emerging in its place is this discipline — part architect, part systems thinker — that I'm calling the Harness Engineer. There's no universal test suite for whether a harness is "good." That judgment still requires human intuition.

That judgment still requires human intuition — and right now, not many people are deliberately building it.
